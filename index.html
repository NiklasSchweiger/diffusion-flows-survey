Diffusion, Flows and Beyond

The lifecycle of a generative model, from pretraining to inference-time search, in seventeen animations.

Niklas SchweigerOctober 2026Read the draft (PDF, 125 pages)

This page accompanies a survey of diffusion and flow models. It follows a model through five stages: pretraining, adaptation, alignment, compression and search. Each stage has one or more animations, and each animation links to the section of the paper that treats it in full.

Most animations use two-dimensional Gaussian mixtures. For a mixture of Gaussians the score, the velocity field and the posterior mean are available in closed form at every noise level, so the arrows and trajectories you see are the exact fields and not the output of a trained network. Where an animation uses something else, such as a trained network, a hand-written model or a schematic, the caption says so. The animations run in your browser from the same code that renders the figures in the paper. The fundamentals of Chapter 2 also have interactive tutorials, which derive each result step by step next to a plot you can change.

Notation. Time runs from noise to data. At \(t=0\) the state is noise and at \(t=1\) it is data, with \(x_t=\alpha_t x_1+\sigma_t x_0\), \(x_0\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\) and \(x_1\) drawn from the data. Score-based diffusion and many image codebases use the opposite direction.

Controls. An animation plays while it is on screen. Click the frame or the play button to pause, drag the slider to scrub, and use the chapter buttons to jump. After clicking a figure, the arrow keys step one second. On a narrow screen the text inside each frame is small, and the caption below it describes the same content.

One distribution connects the later stages. For a pretrained model \(p\), a reward \(r\) and a regularization weight \(\beta>0\), the distribution that maximizes the expected reward minus \(\beta\) times the KL divergence from \(p\) is the tilted distribution

\[ p^\star(x)=\frac{1}{Z}\,p(x)\,e^{r(x)/\beta},\qquad Z=\mathbb E_p\big[e^{r(x)/\beta}\big]. \]

Reward fine-tuning changes the weights of the model so that it samples from \(p^\star\). Guidance and stochastic optimal control change the drift of the sampler instead. Sequential Monte Carlo reaches \(p^\star\) by reweighting and resampling particles during sampling, and best-of-\(N\) reaches a closely related distribution by selection. Conditioning has the same form: with \(\beta=1\) and \(r(x)=\log p(y\mid x)\) the tilt is Bayes' rule. The routes differ in when the compute is spent and in what they need from the reward.

Stage 01 · Pretraining

Learning to move noise to data

Flow matching trains a velocity field \(v_\theta(x,t)\) by regression. A noise sample \(x_0\) and a data sample \(x_1\) define a straight path \(x_t=t\,x_1+(1-t)\,x_0\), whose velocity is \(x_1-x_0\). The loss is

\[ \mathcal L(\theta)=\mathbb E_{t,\,x_0,\,x_1}\,\big\lVert v_\theta(x_t,t)-(x_1-x_0)\big\rVert^2 , \]

and its minimizer is the marginal velocity: the average of \(x_1-x_0\) over all pairs whose path passes through \(x_t\) at time \(t\). The straight paths of individual pairs cross each other. The paths of the marginal field cannot cross, because an ODE has a single velocity at each point, so they bend. Integrating the marginal field from noise produces samples from the data distribution, and an SDE with a matched noise term produces the same marginals along random paths.

Scores and Langevin dynamics

Score-based models predate flow matching. They learn the score \(\nabla_x\log p_\sigma(x)\) of the data smoothed by Gaussian noise of scale \(\sigma\) and sample with Langevin dynamics, \(x\leftarrow x+h\,\nabla_x\log p_\sigma(x)+\sqrt{2h}\,\xi\). At a small \(\sigma\) the score is accurate near the data, but Langevin dynamics moves mass between separated regions very slowly. Annealing \(\sigma\) from large to small values first places the particles at a coarse scale and then refines them. The same idea, a continuum of noise levels, is what the SDE and flow formulations make precise.

From pixels to tokens

Large image and video models do not denoise pixels directly. An autoencoder compresses the image into a smaller latent, the latent is cut into patches, and each patch becomes one token of a transformer. The number of tokens sets the cost of attention, which grows with the square of the sequence length. In MMDiT-style models the text tokens and the image tokens share one attention sequence, so each word can read from every patch.

Diffusion over discrete tokens

Masked diffusion models for language start from a fully masked sequence and reveal tokens over several steps. At each step the model predicts a distribution for every masked position given the visible tokens. Revealing several positions at once is what makes decoding parallel, but the revealed tokens are sampled independently of each other. When two positions depend on each other, independent sampling can pick a combination that never occurs in the data.

Guidance

Classifier-free guidance combines a conditional and an unconditional prediction, \(v_w=v_u+w\,(v_c-v_u)\), and samples with \(w>1\). This extrapolates away from the unconditional model and makes samples more typical of the condition, but it also removes parts of the conditional distribution. Autoguidance keeps the same formula and replaces the unconditional model by a weaker version of the conditional model, The weaker model makes the same kind of errors more strongly, so extrapolating away from it reduces those errors.

Stage 02 · Adaptation

Changing a large model with few parameters

A pretrained model is adapted to a new style, subject or form of control far more often than it is trained. Low-rank adaptation (LoRA) freezes a weight matrix \(W\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}\) and trains a product \(BA\) of a \(d\times r\) and an \(r\times d\) matrix, so the adapted weight \(W+BA\) differs from \(W\) by a matrix of rank at most \(r\). For \(r\ll d\) this trains \(2dr\) numbers instead of \(d^2\). Whether a low-rank update suffices depends on the change. The animation shows a case where it does, with a real trained network.

Stage 03 · Alignment

Fine-tuning towards a reward

Alignment moves the model towards samples that score well under a reward, usually a learned model of human preference or an automatic check such as text rendering. With a KL penalty of weight \(\beta\) the optimal model is the tilted distribution \(p^\star\) from the introduction. For a Gaussian mixture and a linear reward the tilt has a closed form, which makes it possible to watch what happens as \(\beta\) decreases. A learned reward is only a proxy, and when the KL term is weak the model finds regions where the proxy is high and the data are absent.

Policy gradients through the sampler

Policy-gradient methods need the likelihood of each sampling step, which a deterministic ODE does not provide. Flow-GRPO replaces the ODE by an SDE with the same marginals, so each Euler–Maruyama step is a Gaussian transition with a known density. For each prompt it draws a group of samples and uses the reward of each sample relative to the group as its advantage, \(A_i=(r_i-\bar r)/\operatorname{std}(r)\), which removes the need for a learned value function.

Stage 04 · Compression

From forty steps to one

Integrating a learned ODE takes tens of network evaluations per sample. Distillation trains a new network, the student, that reaches the distribution of the teacher in one or a few steps. The methods differ in what the student learns: the endpoint of the teacher's trajectory, a jump between any two times, a straighter field, or a distribution that only has to match the teacher's as a whole.

Flow maps

A flow map \(X_{s,t}\) sends the state at time \(s\) to the state at time \(t\) along the teacher's ODE. Consistency models learn the special case \(X_{s,1}\), which maps every point to the endpoint of its trajectory. MeanFlow learns the average velocity over an interval, from which the jump follows. A single Euler step instead follows the instantaneous velocity at \(t=0\), which points to the mean of the data.

Reflow

Flow matching with independent noise–data pairs learns curved paths. Reflow samples noise, runs the trained ODE to get its endpoint, and trains a new field on these coupled pairs. The new pairs do not cross, so the refitted field has nearly straight paths and few Euler steps suffice.

Distribution matching

Distribution matching distillation (DMD) does not follow trajectories at all. It moves each generator sample along the difference between two scores at a random noise level: the score of the noised data, given by the teacher, and the score of the noised generator samples, given by a second network trained on the generator's own outputs. This difference estimates the gradient of the reverse KL divergence. Without the second score, the update is score distillation sampling, which pulls every sample to the nearest mode.

Causal video and exposure bias

Real-time video models generate one frame or chunk at a time, conditioned on the frames before. When such a model is trained only on real context frames (teacher forcing), its loss says nothing about how it behaves on its own slightly wrong frames. Small errors then compound over a long rollout. Self Forcing trains on the model's own rollouts, so the loss sees the contexts the model will meet at inference.

Stage 05 · Search

Spending compute at inference time

A trained model can also be improved per sample, by spending more compute against a verifier. Best-of-\(N\) draws \(N\) independent samples and keeps the highest-scoring one. Its expected gain grows only like \(\sqrt{2\log N}\) times the standard deviation of the reward, so a reward region that the base model rarely visits stays out of reach. Sequential Monte Carlo steers a population of particles during sampling by reweighting and resampling them. Noise search treats the deterministic sampler as a black box from initial noise to sample and optimizes the noise.

Inverse problems

An inverse problem asks for samples from the posterior \(p(x\mid y)\) given a measurement \(y\), with a pretrained model as the prior. This is the tilt with \(\beta=1\) and \(r(x)=\log p(y\mid x)\). Practical methods such as diffusion posterior sampling (DPS) approximate the guidance term by evaluating the likelihood at the denoised estimate. For a Gaussian mixture prior and a linear Gaussian measurement the posterior is again a mixture, so the approximation can be compared with the exact answer.

Applications

World models and protein backbones

Most applications combine several stages. Two of them raise questions that the toy problems above do not cover. An interactive world model generates the next frame from the current frame and an action, and it has to stay consistent over hundreds of its own outputs. A protein backbone is a chain of rigid residues, so its state space is not \(\mathbb R^d\) but a product of rotations and translations.

Outlook

Open problems

The last section of the paper collects questions that cut across the stages. Each is stated in full there, with the small calculation that makes the trade-off precise.

  1. Autoregression and diffusion as one family

    Block diffusion factorizes a sequence autoregressively over blocks and runs discrete diffusion inside each block. A block of one token is an autoregressive model, and a single block is a masked diffusion model. Under a fixed amount of unique data, masked diffusion keeps improving with repeated epochs and eventually overtakes autoregression. Which properties belong to the factorization and which to the training setup is still unclear.

    §8.1 p. 83

  2. Evaluation that tracks human judgement

    FID can disagree with human raters, and prompt-level benchmarks saturate as models improve. The same preference models serve as rewards, as verifiers and as metrics, so a model trained against a reward and evaluated with it is graded by its own objective. Evaluation needs held-out judges in the same sense in which learning needs held-out data.

    §8.2 p. 84

  3. Reward over-optimization

    Write \(p_\lambda\propto p\,e^{\lambda r}\) with \(\lambda=1/\beta\). For any function \(f\), \(\tfrac{d}{d\lambda}\mathbb E_{p_\lambda}[f]=\operatorname{Cov}_{p_\lambda}(f,r)\). With \(f=r\) this is a variance, so the proxy reward rises monotonically as the regularization weakens. With \(f\) the true quality, the covariance can turn negative once the tilt concentrates where the proxy's error is largest. The KL term limits how far the model moves, not in which direction.

    §8.3 p. 84

  4. Data provenance and memorization

    The minimizer of the denoising loss on a finite training set reproduces the training images, so generalization comes from inductive biases and optimization rather than from the objective. Watermarks answer the reverse question with a hypothesis test. Their interaction with noise search, distillation and open-weight fine-tuning is rarely tested.

    §8.4 p. 85

  5. Compute across the lifecycle

    For a model that produces \(M\) samples, the total cost is \(C_{\mathrm{pre}}+C_{\mathrm{post}}+D+M\,N\,K\,f\), with distillation cost \(D\), \(N\) candidates per output, \(K\) steps and cost \(f\) per step. Distilling from \(K\) to \(k\) steps pays for itself when \(MN(K-k)f>D\). A joint scaling law for all five quantities, measured on a metric that people agree with, does not exist yet.

    §8.5 p. 86

  6. Beyond iterative sampling

    Transition models learn the map over any finite interval and can be sampled with any number of steps. Drifting models move the iteration into training, so that inference takes one network evaluation by design. Much of the lifecycle assumes an iterative sampler with intermediate states, including policy gradients through the SDE, sequential Monte Carlo and classifier-free guidance, and it is open how these methods carry over.

    §8.6 p. 87
Tutorials · Chapter 2

Deriving the fundamentals step by step

Each tutorial follows one section of Chapter 2. A step states a result, shows it in a plot you can change and derives it one line at a time, with a hint for every line that names the rule it uses. As in the animations, the targets are Gaussian mixtures, so every density, score and velocity in the plots is exact.

§2.08 steps

Data and distributions

A primer for the chapter. It shows how an image, a molecule, a protein and a sentence become vectors, and how a set of samples is described by a density. It fits a Gaussian by maximum likelihood, shows that this is the same as minimizing a KL divergence, and ends with a mixture fitted by EM.
§2.17 steps

Energies, scores and Langevin dynamics

An energy defines a density, and its score does not depend on the normalizing constant. Langevin dynamics samples with the score alone. The last steps show why one noise level is not enough and how annealing the noise recovers the right mass per mode.
§2.27 steps

Learning the score: from Hyvärinen to Tweedie

How a model learns the score from samples when the density is unknown. The tutorial goes from explicit and implicit score matching to denoising score matching, shows that the marginal score is an average of conditional scores, and ends with Tweedie’s formula and the four prediction targets.
§2.37 steps

Diffusion as an SDE

Letting the number of noise levels go to infinity turns the corruption into a stochastic differential equation. The tutorial derives the Fokker–Planck equation, the reverse-time SDE and the probability-flow ODE, and writes both samplers in the survey’s interpolation convention.
§2.47 steps

Flows: the continuity equation and flow matching

From the continuity equation to the flow-matching loss, trained live in the page. The velocity and the score turn out to be the same field, one trained velocity supports a whole family of samplers, and straighter paths need fewer steps.
§2.57 steps

The likelihood view: ELBO, VDM and loss weighting

A diffusion model read as a deep VAE. The tutorial derives the variational bound, its continuous-time limit and why the noise schedule only changes its variance. It then writes common losses as weightings of one integrand and computes exact likelihoods with the probability-flow ODE.
§2.67 steps

Couplings, transport and control

A diffusion model is trained on pairs of noise and data points, and the pairing can be chosen. The tutorial compares random and optimal pairings by their paths, their sampling error and their kinetic energy, then covers rectified flow and the Schrödinger bridge. It ends with reward fine-tuning as stochastic optimal control and compares the exact control with guidance and SMC.
§2.76 steps

Why diffusion models generalize

The exact minimizer of the training loss only reproduces the training set. The tutorial samples from this optimum, measures when along the path it commits to a single training point, and compares two models trained on disjoint data. It then shows which points a smoothed model still copies and how a local, shift-equivariant denoiser composes new digits from patches.
§2.87 steps

Representations and architectures

An autoencoder and a patch size decide how many tokens a model sees, and the noise schedule has to shift with the resolution. The tutorial shows a perfect autoencoder whose latent space a diffusion model still fills badly, derives the target of representation alignment, and compares which prediction target a narrow network can learn. It ends with attention as the empirical denoiser and with the cost of joint text and image attention.
§2.96 steps

Discrete and language diffusion

Diffusion on tokens replaces the noise by a Markov chain that masks or resamples them. The tutorial computes the reverse rates of this chain exactly, shows that they need the ratio of probabilities instead of the score, and derives the masked-diffusion bound on a toy language whose likelihood is known. It ends with discrete flow matching and with the errors that come from revealing several tokens in one step.
§2.106 steps

Autoregressive and hybrid visual generation

An image can be generated pixel by pixel, scale by scale or by denoising, and each choice trades sequential steps against the dependence that one step can capture. The tutorial computes every conditional of a toy image distribution exactly, shows where parallel steps produce mixed digits, and measures what a discrete codebook loses. It ends with a diffusion head for continuous tokens and with one transformer that trains on text and images together.
§2.116 steps

Conditioning and guidance

A conditional model can be steered by a classifier or by the difference of two of its own predictions. The tutorial computes every score and classifier of a toy mixture exactly, shows that classifier-free guidance does not sample the sharpened density it is derived from, and compares it with the exact correction. It ends with guidance intervals, autoguidance and a particle method that reaches the target.
